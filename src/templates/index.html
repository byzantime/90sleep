What is the 90-minute sleep cycle?

Sleep is not one continuous state. Through the night your brain moves through a repeating sequence of stages, and one pass through that sequence is called a sleep cycle. For most adults a cycle lasts somewhere between about 80 and 120 minutes, so 90 minutes is a convenient average rather than a fixed rule.

Each cycle moves through three broad kinds of sleep:

  • Light sleep (stages N1 and N2). The transition from wakefulness: your heart rate and breathing slow and your body temperature drops. Adults spend roughly half the night here.
  • Deep sleep (stage N3, also called slow-wave sleep). The hardest stage to wake from and the one most associated with physical recovery. Deep sleep is concentrated in the first few cycles of the night.
  • REM sleep. The stage in which most vivid dreaming happens. REM periods are short early in the night and grow longer in later cycles, which is why you often wake from a dream in the morning.

Is everyone's sleep cycle 90 minutes? No. Cycle length varies from person to person and even from one cycle to the next in the same night, and it is affected by age, recent sleep loss, alcohol and caffeine. Treat the 90-minute figure as a useful starting point, and adjust if you find you reliably wake up groggy at the suggested times.

How this sleep cycle calculator works

Waking up in the middle of deep sleep tends to leave you feeling heavy and disoriented, a state known as sleep inertia. Waking near the end of a cycle, when sleep is lighter, is usually easier. The calculator tries to line your alarm up with those lighter moments.

In “I need to wake up at…” mode, it starts from your wake-up time and counts backwards in 90-minute steps, then subtracts a further {{ fall_asleep_minutes }} minutes, roughly how long it takes the average person to fall asleep. The result is the time to be in bed with the lights off.

In “I'm going to bed now” mode, it does the reverse: it adds {{ fall_asleep_minutes }} minutes to the current time and then counts forwards in 90-minute steps to give you a list of wake-up times.

The highlighted results are 4, 5 or 6 cycles (6, 7.5 or 9 hours of sleep), which covers the 7–9 hours generally recommended for adults. Shorter options are shown for nights when time is tight, but they are not a substitute for a full night.

How many sleep cycles is 8 hours? About five and a third. Five full cycles take 7.5 hours, so if you have an 8-hour window, aiming for five cycles plus the time it takes to drift off fits neatly.

How many hours of sleep is 9pm to 5am? That is 8 hours in bed. Allowing around a quarter of an hour to fall asleep leaves about 7¾ hours of actual sleep, which is just over five 90-minute cycles.

Frequently asked questions

How many 90-minute sleep cycles do you need?

Most adults need about five cycles (7.5 hours) a night, and somewhere between four and six cycles (6–9 hours) covers most people. Teenagers and children need more. How you feel during the day is a better guide than any formula.

Is 90 minutes a full sleep cycle?

For many adults it is close, but not exactly. A full cycle usually takes 80–120 minutes, and cycles later in the night tend to be different in make-up from earlier ones. Ninety minutes is a practical average for planning, not a measurement of your own sleep.

Is 4.5 hours of sleep enough?

Three cycles may help you wake up less groggy than an arbitrary short night, but 4.5 hours is well below what adults need. Regularly sleeping this little leads to accumulated sleep debt, so keep it for genuine emergencies.

Is 6 hours of sleep enough?

Six hours is four complete cycles and is a reasonable fallback on a busy night. Over weeks, though, most adults who sleep six hours perform worse than they would with seven or more, often without noticing it themselves.

Is 7.5 hours of sleep enough?

For most adults, yes. Five cycles land within the commonly recommended 7–9 hours, and 7.5 hours is often the sweet spot this calculator points towards.

What is the “90 minute rule” for sleep?

It is the idea of timing your sleep in multiples of 90 minutes, so that your alarm goes off at the end of a cycle rather than in the middle of one. It is a rule of thumb, not a law of biology: it works best alongside a consistent bedtime and enough total sleep.

Is a 90-minute nap a good idea?

A 90-minute nap gives you roughly one complete cycle, including some deep and REM sleep, so you are less likely to wake up mid-cycle than after a 45- or 60-minute nap. If you only have a short window, a 10–20 minute nap that stays in light sleep is the other common choice. Avoid long naps late in the day, as they can make it harder to fall asleep at night.
